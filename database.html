01 · The fundamentals

What is a database?

A database is an organized collection of information that can be stored, searched, changed, and managed.

🗂️

Why databases matter

They keep information organized, reduce repeated data, support accurate updates, and let many authorized users find information efficiently.

⚙️

What is a DBMS?

A Database Management System is software used to create, store, protect, query, and maintain databases. Examples include MySQL, PostgreSQL, and Microsoft SQL Server.

📊

Database vs. spreadsheet

A spreadsheet is useful for smaller lists and calculations. A database is designed for structured data, relationships, controlled access, and many records or users.

Databases are part of everyday life

🎓 Universities

Student schedules, grades, majors, and registration.

🏦 Banks

Accounts, transactions, balances, and customer records.

🛒 Online shopping

Products, orders, payments, and shipping information.

🏥 Hospitals

Appointments, patient records, and test results.

📱 Social media

Profiles, posts, comments, and connections.

📚 Libraries

Books, borrowers, checkouts, and due dates.

03 · Database structure

Tables, records, and relationships

Relational databases split information into tables and connect those tables with keys.

Important building blocks

Table: A collection of related data, such as Students.

Row / record: One complete entry, such as one student.

Column / field: One type of information, such as Name or Major ID.

Primary key: A field that uniquely identifies each row.

Foreign key: A field that refers to a key in another table, connecting the records.

Example: two related tables

The Students table stores MajorID as a foreign key. It refers to MajorID in the Majors table.

05 · Database categories
Different types of databases
Different systems are designed for different kinds of information, scale, and workloads.

  

    
Relational databases
Organize data into structured tables with rows, columns, and relationships. They commonly use SQL and constraints to maintain data integrity.
Advantages: Strong consistency, clear structure, and powerful queries.
Applications: Banking, school systems, inventory, and order processing.
Examples: MySQLPostgreSQLSQL Server

    
NoSQL databases
Use data models such as documents, key-value pairs, graphs, or wide columns rather than requiring one fixed relational table structure.
Advantages: Flexible data models and options for scaling across distributed systems.
Applications: Content platforms, caching, event data, and rapidly changing application data.
Examples: MongoDBRedis

    
Cloud databases
Databases hosted on cloud infrastructure, often with managed backups, scaling, monitoring, and maintenance services.
Advantages: Flexible capacity and access without managing all the hardware yourself.
Applications: Web apps, business software, and services used across locations.
Examples: Amazon RDSAzure SQL

    
Distributed databases
Store or replicate data across multiple connected computers or locations while coordinating how the system handles requests and updates.
Advantages: Can improve availability, geographic reach, and scalability when designed well.
Applications: Global services, large platforms, and systems that need resilience.
Examples: CockroachDBApache Cassandra
